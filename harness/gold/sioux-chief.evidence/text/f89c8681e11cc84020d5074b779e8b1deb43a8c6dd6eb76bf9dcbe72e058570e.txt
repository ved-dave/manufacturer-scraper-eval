Cast Iron Grating & Corrosion
Although all Sioux Chief cast iron drain grates have an enamel/paint coating from the 
factory, the paint coating is not intended to protect the iron material from 'rust' for 
any significant period of time.
Cast iron grates are ideal for heavy-duty loads and high-traffic areas. They are very 
durable and often outlast other components in the drainage system. However, paint 
coatings will wear off, as can be expected on grates that are walked/driven over. The 
cast iron base material will then become orange with rust when exposed to any form 
of moisture - including abient humidity. This is due to oxidation, which may be seen 
as a defect, but is actually perfectly normal – if not completely sealed off from the 
surrounding atmosphere, all cast iron grates will go through the process of oxidation. 
Oxidation occurs when oxygen atoms bond with iron atoms, resulting in the formation 
of iron oxides (rust). 
As the initial rust coating wears away, a layer of iron-oxide silicate is created, due 
to the presence of silicon within the iron. This layer then becomes somewhat 
passive (not actively rusting) and actually protects the grate from becoming further 
corroded. Over a period of time, the oxidized cast iron will darken to a brown-ish color, 
commonly seen on man-hole covers and other examples of 'aged' cast iron.
The hue and timing of the long-term coloration depends on a few factors and can 
take anywhere from a few months to a year. The chemical makeup of the iron can 
affect whether you end up with a reddish-amber shade, or more of a chocolate-brown. 
Other factors are regional and environmental in nature. Areas where the grate may 
be exposed to saltwater can have an impact on the transition as well as whether the 
grates are exposed to harsh chemicals. Because of the variability of these factors, 
the change can occur in as soon as six months or take over two years - and is nearly 
impossible to predict.
